### Classification

* Quality of Service (QoS) purpose is to give certain kinds of traffic a priority over others during congestion.
* **Classification** = organize network traffic (packets) into *traffic classes* (categories)
* Classification is fundamental to QoS. To give priority to certain types of traffic, you have to identify which types of traffic to give priority to.
* There are many methods of classifying traffic. Some examples:
  - **ACL**. Traffic which is permitted by the ACL will be given certain treatment, other traffic will not.
  - **NBAR** (Network Based Application Recognition) performs *deep packet inspection*, looking beyond the Layer 3 and Layer 4 information up to Layer 7 to identify the specific kind of traffic.
  - In the Layer 2 and Layer 3 headers there are specific fields used for this purpose.
* The **PCP** (Priority Code Point) field of the 802.1Q tab (in the Ethernet header) can be used to identify high/low priority traffic. This is only used when there is a dot1q tag!
* The **DSCP** (Differentiated Services Code Point) field of the IP header can also be used to identify high/low priority traffic.

### PCP/CoS

![Pasted image 20260726081113.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726081113.png)

* The default PCP value is 0 (best effort).
* Don't mix Class of Service (CoS) with entire concept of Quality of Service (QoS)! CoS just refers to the PCP of dot1q tag.
* **mark** = to 'mark traffic' means to *set the value* in the **PCP** or **DSCP** field. Then, network devices look at those markings and use them to classify the traffic as high priority, low priority, and vice versa.

Because PCP is found in the dot1q header, it can only be used over the following connections:

* **trunk links**
* **access links with a voice VLAN**

In the diagram below, traffic between R1 and R2, or between R2 and external destinations will not have a dot1q tag. So, traffic over those links PCP cannot be marked with a PCP value.

### IP ToS Byte

![Pasted image 20260726082314.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726082314.png)

* In IPv4, there is a byte that is referred to as **Type of Service (ToS) byte**. In IPv6, there is a label called 'traffic class' which is the IPv6 equivalent of ToS byte. Both are used for QoS.
* In modern ToS byte, it is composed of the field **Differentiated Services Code Point (DSCP)** and **Explicit Congestion Notification (ECN)**.
* 6 bits of the DSCP field is 64 possible values. This gives us flexibility regarding how we can mark and classify the traffic in the network.

---

### IP Precedence

![Pasted image 20260726083117.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726083117.png)

* Standard IPP markings are similar to PCP:
  - 6 and 7 are reserved for 'network control' traffic (ie. OSPF messages between routers)
  - 5 = voice
  - 4 = video
  - 3 = voice signaling
  - 0 = best effortraffic class
* With 6 and 7 reserved, *6 possible values* remain.
* Although 6 values is sufficient for many networks, the QoS requirements of some networks demand more flexibility.

### DSCP

![Pasted image 20260726083459.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726083459.png)

* RFC 2474 (1998) defines the DSCP field, and other 'DiffServ' RFCs elaborate on its use.
* With IPP updated to DSCP, new standard markings had to be decided upon.
  - By having generally agreed upon standard markings for different kinds of traffic
  - QoS design & implementation is simplified
  - QoS works better between ISPs and enterprises
  - etc.
* You should be aware of the following standard markings:
  - Default Forwarding (DF) - best effort traffic
  - Expedited Forwarding (EF) - low loss/latency/jitter traffic (usually voice)
  - Assured Forwarding (AF) - A set of 12 standard values
  - Class Selector (CS) - A set of 8 standard values, provides backward compatibility with IPP

* Within each class, there are three levels of *drop precedence*.

### RFC 4954

* RFC 4954 was developed with the help of Cisco to bring all the mentioned values together and standardize their use.
* The RFC offers many specific recommendations, but here are a few key ones:
  - Voice traffic: **EF**
  - Interactive video: **AF4x**
  - Streaming video: **AF3x**
  - High priority data: **AF2x**
  - Best effort: **DF**
* There are many more recommendations, you can google search for those other recommendations for RFC 4954.

### Trust Boundaries

* The *trust boundary* of a network defines where devices trust/don't trust the QoS markings of received messages.
* If the markings are trusted, the device will forward the message without changing the markings.
* If the markings aren't trusted, the device will change the markings according to the configured policy.

![Pasted image 20260726100803.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726100803.png)

* SW1 doesn't trust the markings from PH1 because it's from outside the trust boundary.
* Perhaps it changes the DSCP markings (from EF to DF), and the CoS markings (from CoS5 to CoS0). Since R1 doesn't have dot1q tag, CoS (PCP) is not used once it reached R1.
* The above diagram is *not ideal*, because usually it's best to trust the markings from an IP phone. We want IP phones traffic to have high priority.

![Pasted image 20260726101549.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726101549.png)

* If an IP phone is connected to the switch port, it is recommended to move the trust boundary to the IP phones.
* This is done via configuration on the switch port connected to the IP phone (not directly on the phone itself).
* If a user marks their PC's traffic with a high priority, the marking will be changed (not trusted)

### Queuing/Congestion Management

![ 500](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726112001.png)

* When a network device receives traffic faster than it can forward the traffic out of the appropriate interface, packets are placed in that interface's queue as they wait to be forwarded.
* When the queue becomes full, packets that don't fit in the queue are dropped (tail drop).
* RED and WRED drop packets early to avoid tail drop.

![Pasted image 20260726112219.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726112219.png)

* An essential part of QoS is the use of multiple queues.
  - This is where classification plays a role. The device can match traffic based on various factors (for example the DSCP marking in the IP header) and then place it in the appropriate queue.
* However, the device is only able to forward one frame out of an interface at once, so a *scheduler* is used to decide which queue traffic is forwarded from next.
  - *Prioritization* allows the scheduler to give certain queue more priority than others.

![Pasted image 20260726112538.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726112538.png)

* A common scheduling method is *weighted round-robin*
  - **round-robin** = packets are taken from each queue in order, cyclically
  - **weighted** = more data is taken from high priority queues each time the scheduler reaches that queue
* **CBWFQ** (Class-Based Weighted Fair Queuing) is a popular method of scheduling, using a weighted round-robin scheduler while guaranteeing each queue a certain percentage of the interface's bandwidth during congestion.
* <u>Round-robin scheduling is not ideal for voice/video traffic.</u> Even if the voice/video traffic receives a guaranteed minimum amount of bandwidth, round-robin can add delay and jitter because even the high priority queues have to wait their turn in the scheduler.

![Pasted image 20260726113037.png](../_assets/Pasted%20image%2020260726113037.png)

* **LLQ** (Low Latency Queuing) designates one (or more) queues as *strict priority queues*.
  - This means that if there is traffic in the queue, the scheduler will <u>always</u> take the next packet from that queue until it is empty.
* This is very effective for reducing the delay and jitter of voice/video traffic.
* However, it has the downside of potentially starving other queues if there is always traffic in the designated strict priority queue.
  - *Policing* can control the amount of traffic allowed in the strict priority queue so that it can't take all of the link's bandwidth.

### Shaping and Policing

* Traffic **shaping** and **policing** are both used to control the rate of traffic.
* **Shaping** buffers traffic in a queue if the traffic rate goes over the configured rate.
* **Policing** drops traffic if the traffic rate goes over the configured rate. However, policing also has the option of re-marking the traffic instead of dropping it.
  - 'Burst' traffic over the configured rate is allowed for a short period of time.
  - This accommodates data applications which typically are 'bursty' in nature. Instead of a constant stream of data, they send data in bursts.
  - The amount of burst traffic allowed is configurable.
* In both cases, classification can be used to allow for different rates for different kinds of traffic.
